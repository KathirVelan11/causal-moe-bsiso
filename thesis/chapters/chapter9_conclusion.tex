\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conc-summary}

This thesis addressed a gap left by existing graph Mixture-of-Experts methods, which route by topology or curvature, keep experts frozen, rely on hand-constructed shift types or add experts on a schedule: none combines causal structure discovery with drift-triggered expert adaptation for spatiotemporal weather forecasting. In Phase~1, a causal graph of lagged climate dependencies was discovered with a verified, vectorised PCMCI over 300 nodes and 60 lags, validated by four independent checks, frozen, and used to guide per-region causal splitters and experts forecasting BSISO-season OLR. A dual-channel drift detector and an expert lifecycle kept each region's expert current without retraining the whole model.

\section{Principal Findings}
\label{sec:conc-findings}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The causal graph is trustworthy by every check that can reject it.} No decoy was selected, almost no link needs an impossible speed, most links survive a change of resolution, and for region 22 the graph and the independently trained splitter select the same drivers.
  \item \textbf{The graph makes source selection meaningful.} In a large candidate pool, where the unguided splitter cannot discriminate, the graph prior yields informative selections and improves 1-day skill in most regions.
  \item \textbf{Time scales must match.} The validated links act within one to two days, so the graph helps at short leads and is neutral at 7 days; week-ahead guidance requires a graph built with lags of at least the forecast lead.
  \item \textbf{Causal structure can reveal drift early.} At two of three regions, the structure channel detected known shifts several times sooner than the error channel.
  \item \textbf{Regimes recur, and archived experts pay off.} Most regime changes reactivated an archived expert, and blending the best three archived experts reduced both forecast error and forgetting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conc-limitations}

The causal claims rest on indirect validation rather than ground truth and assume that common drivers are observed. The forecasting comparisons use one training seed and are made against the no-prior model; the drift and lifecycle results cover three regions; and the data come from a single archive whose climatology overlaps the test years by two years.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:conc-future}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compare the causal prior with random and lagged-correlation priors of equal size, and run the initialise and regularise modes and all pools, with several seeds.
  \item Rebuild the graph with lags of at least seven days for week-ahead forecasting.
  \item Extend the drift and lifecycle evaluation to all 50 regions and to strength-based structural signals.
  \item Test the graph on a second reanalysis or OLR product and on a refitted climatology.
  \item Implement Phase~2 over India and benchmark it against GraphCast and WeatherNext; then scale to global forecasting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:conc-final}

Validating causal drivers before using them, and adapting only the regions whose behaviour has measurably changed, gives a forecasting system that is easier to trust and cheaper to keep current than one that is retrained in full. Phase~1 shows that each element of this approach works on a real tropical forecasting problem and identifies what must change, chiefly matching the graph's time scale to the forecast lead, as the system moves to hourly regional forecasting over India.
