\section{Why the Discovered Graph Requires Independent Validation}
\label{sec:val-why}

PCMCI with FDR control limits the expected fraction of false links under its assumptions. Those assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}) are only approximately met, and the FDR guarantee says nothing about whether a link is physically possible, whether it is stable under a different spatial aggregation, or whether a graph of that size is useful to a forecaster. There is also no ground truth for the real atmosphere against which a discovered graph could be scored. Validation must therefore rely on checks that can \emph{reject} a bad graph even though no check can \emph{prove} a graph correct.

Stage~B applies four such checks, in a fixed order, to the Stage~A graph (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}):
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{B\arabic*}]
  \item \textbf{Surrogate decoys:} is the pipeline better than chance at rejecting series that have realistic autocorrelation but no real coupling?
  \item \textbf{Physical lag mask:} is each link's implied propagation speed achievable by a process carried by its source variable?
  \item \textbf{Data-driven top-$k$:} how many outside links per target are worth keeping?
  \item \textbf{Resolution persistence:} do the links reappear when the regions are redrawn at twice the resolution?
\end{enumerate}
The order runs from the cheapest and most general check to the most specific. B1 tests the whole pipeline before any link is trusted; B2 removes physically impossible links before they influence the ranking in B3; B3 sets the size of the prior; and B4, which needs a second full PCMCI run, flags links that depend on the partition. The resulting graph is then frozen and used without further change.

\section{Check B1: Surrogate Decoys}
\label{sec:val-b1}

\subsection{Rationale and null model}
A climate series can be selected as a parent because it is genuinely coupled to the target, or because its autocorrelation and spectral peaks make chance alignment with the target likely. The B1 check asks how often the full pipeline (PC1, MCI and FDR together) selects series that share the spectral properties of real nodes but are, by construction, uncoupled from everything. Such series are \emph{decoys}: they are placed in the candidate pool alongside the real nodes, so they must be rejected by the same tests under the same conditions.

The null model is a stationary linear Gaussian process with the same power spectrum as a real node~\cite{theiler1992testing,schreiber2000surrogate}. A phase-randomised surrogate of a series $x_t$ of length $n$ is generated by Fourier-transforming the demeaned series, replacing the phases with independent uniform random phases, and transforming back:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_f = \sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(x_t-\bar{x})\,e^{-2\pi i f t/n},\qquad
  \tilde{x}_t = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{f}|\hat{x}_f|\,e^{i\theta_f}\,e^{2\pi i f t/n},\qquad
  \theta_f\sim\mathcal{U}[0,2\pi),
  \label{eq:phaserand}
\end{equation}
with $\theta_0=0$ (and $\theta_{n/2}=0$ for even $n$) so that $\tilde{x}_t$ is real, and $\theta_{-f}=-\theta_f$ by construction of the real inverse transform. The surrogate is then standardised. Because $|\hat{x}_f|$ is unchanged, the surrogate has the same periodogram and therefore the same sample autocorrelation function as the original, including its 30--60-day intraseasonal peak. Because the phases are random, its cross-spectrum with any other series has random phase, so its expected lagged cross-covariance with every real node is zero.

\subsection{What phase randomisation preserves and what it does not}
The surrogate preserves the power spectrum, the autocorrelation function and the variance of the original series. It does not preserve:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{the amplitude distribution:} surrogates are close to Gaussian even when the original is skewed, as convective OLR anomalies are;
  \item \textbf{nonstationarity:} the seasonal modulation of variance and of the BSISO's amplitude is spread uniformly over the record;
  \item \textbf{nonlinear structure:} any dependence on phase relations between frequencies;
  \item \textbf{cross-variable structure:} a decoy built from one region's u850 has no relation to that region's other fields, whereas real fields of one region move together.
\end{itemize}
Amplitude-adjusted and iterated surrogates~\cite{schreiber2000surrogate,lancaster2018surrogate} can restore the amplitude distribution. The other limitations mean that decoys are an easier null than the most dangerous real false positives, which are series coupled to the target only through an unobserved common driver. A low decoy rate therefore shows that the pipeline does not select series merely because of their autocorrelation; it does not eliminate confounding and does not prove that the real links are causal.

\subsection{Construction and separate pass}
$D=250$ decoys are generated. For each, a real node is drawn at random (with replacement) from the source nodes and phase-randomised with an independent random generator, and the decoy is labelled with its origin. The decoy run is a \emph{separate} PCMCI pass with a pool of $300+250=550$ nodes, which gives $550\times 60=33{,}000$ candidates per target. Running it separately keeps the main graph free of any effect of the decoys on the FDR correction (the number of hypotheses changes) and on the PC1 condition ordering. In this pass, decoys are allowed only their own past as parents, like other non-target nodes.

\subsection{Statistics}
Let $\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ be the links selected in the decoy pass and $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sel}}$ the set of decoys that appear as a parent of at least one target. The \emph{decoy rate} is
\begin{equation}
  \delta = \frac{|\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sel}}|}{D}.
  \label{eq:decoyrate}
\end{equation}
The \emph{decoy edge share} is the fraction of selected links in the decoy pass whose source is a decoy. The strongest selected decoy defines a strength threshold
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_{\mathrm{dec}} = \max_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{dec}}:\,\mathrm{src}(e)\in\mathcal{D}} |\MCI_e|,
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
and a real link passes B1 if $|\MCI_e|>\kappa_{\mathrm{dec}}$. If no decoy is selected, $\kappa_{\mathrm{dec}}=0$ and the strength comparison is satisfied by every real link; in that case B1's evidence is carried by the decoy rate and by the stability of the real graph between the clean pass and the decoy pass. A more informative comparison, recommended for future runs, is between the distribution of real-link MCI strengths and the distribution of decoy MCI strengths recorded \emph{before} FDR, which is non-degenerate even when no decoy survives.

\subsection{The acceptance line}
The project plan sets a decoy rate of 30\% as a warning line. This value is a pragmatic, project-specific threshold and not a statistical standard. Under the decoy null, every decoy is a false positive by construction, so with FDR control at 5\% the expected decoy selection rate is low, and a rate approaching 30\% would indicate that the tests are badly miscalibrated for series with realistic autocorrelation. The threshold was fixed before the decoy pass was run, and its role is to stop the pipeline if it fails, not to certify the graph if it passes.

\section{Check B2: Physical Lag Masking}
\label{sec:val-b2}

\subsection{Distance, lag and implied speed}
A link from source region $r'$ to target region $r$ at lag $\tau$ requires a signal to travel at least the distance between the two regions within $\tau$ days. The distance is taken as the great-circle distance between the \emph{closest} pair of cells of the two regions, computed with the haversine formula~\cite{sinnott1984virtues}:
\begin{equation}
  d(r',r) = \min_{c'\in\Omega_{r'},\,c\in\Omega_r} 2a\,\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\!\tfrac{\varphi_c-\varphi_{c'}}{2}+\cos\varphi_{c'}\cos\varphi_c\sin^2\!\tfrac{\lambda_c-\lambda_{c'}}{2}},
  \label{eq:haversine}
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ and $\lambda$ are latitude and longitude and $a$ is the Earth's radius. For adjacent regions $d=0$. The implied propagation speed is
\begin{equation}
  s_e = \frac{d(r',r)}{\tau\,\Delta t},\qquad \Delta t = 86{,}400\ \mathrm{s}.
  \label{eq:speed}
\end{equation}
Because the closest-cell distance is a lower bound on any path between the regions, $s_e$ is a lower bound on the speed that the link requires. A link \emph{fails} B2 if
\begin{equation}
  s_e > s^{\max}_{v_{\mathrm{src}}},
\end{equation}
where $s^{\max}_v$ is the maximum speed at which a process carried by the source variable $v$ can plausibly propagate.

\subsection{Speed limits by source variable}
The limits are deliberately generous. Table~\ref{tab:speeds} lists them together with their physical justification. They are guidelines that encode the order of magnitude of the relevant processes, not universal constants.

\subsection{Distance cutoff versus speed constraint}
A geographic-distance cutoff would remove all links between regions more than some fixed distance apart. That would wrongly remove teleconnections: a Kelvin wave can carry an upper-level wind signal 4{,}000\,km in about a day. A speed constraint instead removes only links that are too \emph{fast}: the same 4{,}000\,km at lag 1 is admissible for u200 (46\ms) but not for SST. Remote links at long enough lags, and all links between adjacent regions, pass automatically.

\subsection{Oscillation aliasing}
Because the BSISO is quasi-periodic, a real link at lag $\tau$ can reappear at lags $\tau\pm P$ where $P$ is the 30--60-day period, since the source is then in a similar phase. Links at $\tau\ge 25$ days are therefore \emph{flagged} as possible aliases of the oscillation. A flag does not remove the link; it marks it for inspection.

\subsection{Diagnostic versus exclusion}
A B2 failure does not prove that the link is spurious. It may reflect an incorrect lag (the true coupling is at a longer lag that was absorbed by conditioning), an unobserved relay (the signal travels through intermediate regions whose effect is distributed), or an oscillatory alias. Failed links are therefore retained in the frozen graph file with their computed distance, speed and failure flag, so that they can be inspected. For forecasting, however, a link that requires an impossible speed should not drive source selection, so B2 failures are excluded from the region-level prior. This separates the scientific record (all links with all diagnostics) from the operational prior (only physically admissible links).

\section{Check B3: Data-Driven Top-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} Selection}
\label{sec:val-b3}

\subsection{Purpose}
A forecasting prior should be small enough to concentrate the splitter on a few sources, but not so small that useful information is lost. B3 ranks the surviving outside links of each target and keeps the $k$ strongest. Own-region links are not counted against the budget: the target region is always a candidate for its own forecaster, so the prior concerns only which \emph{other} regions to add.

\subsection{Ranking}
Links are ranked by $|\MCI|$, the conditional effect size, rather than by $q$-value. A $q$-value measures how confidently a link is non-zero, which depends heavily on sample size; $|\MCI|$ measures how much unique information the source contributes. Other possible measures are the drop in validation error when the link is removed, or bootstrap selection frequency. They are more expensive and could be used in a refined version of the check.

\subsection{Choosing \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} from development data}
For each candidate $k\in\{0,\dots,k_{\max}\}$, a ridge regression~\cite{hoerl1970ridge} is fitted for each target on 1979--2004 season days, using as predictors all own-region links plus the top-$k$ outside links, each at its own lag:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm\beta}_k = \arg\min_{\bm\beta}\;\sum_{t\in\Tseason\cap[1979,2004]}\Big(X^j_t-\beta_0-\!\!\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{own}}_j\cup\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out},k}_j}\!\!\beta_e X^{\mathrm{src}(e)}_{t-\tau_e}\Big)^2+\epsilon\lVert\bm\beta\rVert^2,
\end{equation}
with a small ridge constant $\epsilon=10^{-3}$ for numerical stability. Each fit is scored by the coefficient of determination on 2005--2012 season days,
\begin{equation}
  R^2_{\mathrm{val}}(k) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left(1-\frac{\var\big(X^j_t-\hat X^{j}_{t,k}\big)}{\var\big(X^j_t\big)}\right),\qquad t\in\Tseason\cap[2005,2012],
  \label{eq:r2k}
\end{equation}
averaged over targets, and $k^{\ast}=\arg\max_k R^2_{\mathrm{val}}(k)$ with $k_{\max}=10$. The test period plays no part. Because the regression uses each link at its own lag and evaluates the target at time $t$, this check measures the in-sample explanatory value of the links for the target at their own time scale, not their value at a fixed forecast lead.

\subsection{Limits of top-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}}
Keeping the $k$ strongest links does not make them more causal; it only bounds the size of the prior. If PCMCI with FDR already produces a sparse graph, B3 may keep every outside link, in which case it acts as a check that no target has an excessive number of drivers rather than as a filter. A selection criterion matched to the forecast lead, or one that requires the selected links to beat random links of the same size, would make B3 more discriminating and is part of the future-work programme.

\section{Check B4: Resolution Persistence}
\label{sec:val-b4}

\subsection{Why aggregation matters}
Region means are averages of many cells. Changing the partition changes the averaged series: smaller regions have more variance, lower autocorrelation and weaker co-variability with remote regions. A link that is an artefact of how two particular regions were drawn should be fragile under a different partition, whereas a link that reflects a large-scale process should survive. B4 tests this.

\subsection{Nested partition and correspondence}
Each of the 50 regions is split into two children by $k$-means with $k=2$ on its own cells, which gives 100 regions numbered so that the children of region $r$ are $2r$ and $2r+1$. The full Stage~A pipeline is rerun on the 100-region system (596 nodes, $596\times 60=35{,}760$ candidates per target, the same 7{,}153 season days). The 300-node and 596-node graphs cannot be compared by raw node index; the nesting provides the correspondence rule $\mathrm{parent}(c)=\lfloor c/2\rfloor$. An outside link $e=(r',v,\tau)\to r$ at 50 regions \emph{persists} if, for some child target $c\in\{2r,2r+1\}$, the fine graph contains a link $(c',v',\tau')\to c$ with
\begin{equation}
  \lfloor c'/2\rfloor = r',\qquad v'=v,\qquad |\tau'-\tau|\le 2.
  \label{eq:persist}
\end{equation}
The lag tolerance of $\pm 2$ days allows for the slightly different timing of the signal at a sub-region. The persistence score of target $r$ is the fraction of its validated outside links that persist,
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_r = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_r|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_r}\mathbb{1}[e\ \text{persists}],
  \label{eq:persistscore}
\end{equation}
undefined for targets with no outside link. A target passes if $\Pi_r\ge 0.5$. Failures are flagged, not deleted.

\subsection{Interpretation}
Persistence supports robustness but does not prove causality: a link driven by a large-scale unobserved common driver will also persist. Conversely, non-persistence can be a genuine scale effect: a link carried by a coherent large-scale signal may be too diluted at sub-region scale to pass FDR with the larger candidate pool (35{,}760 instead of 18{,}000 hypotheses per target), or the relevant source signal may sit in only one child region whose link to the target falls just below the threshold. This is why B4 flags rather than deletes.

\section{Graph Comparison and Robustness Metrics}
\label{sec:val-metrics}

Several stages compare two link sets $A$ and $B$, for example the clean and decoy passes in B1, the two resolutions in B4, or consecutive windows in Stage~D. The following metrics are used or recommended:
\begin{align}
  \text{Jaccard similarity:}\quad & J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}, \label{eq:jaccard}\\
  \text{Persistence (directional):}\quad & \Pi(A\to B)=\frac{|\{a\in A: \exists b\in B,\;a\simeq b\}|}{|A|}, \label{eq:directional}\\
  \text{Lag stability:}\quad & \Delta\tau = \operatorname{median}_{a\simeq b}|\tau_a-\tau_b|, \\
  \text{Strength stability:}\quad & \mathrm{corr}\big(|\MCI_a|,|\MCI_b|\big)\ \text{over matched pairs}\ a\simeq b,
\end{align}
where $a\simeq b$ denotes the correspondence rule of the comparison. The directional persistence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:directional} is used in B4 because the fine graph is expected to contain more links than the coarse graph. Jaccard similarity on (source region, field) sets defines the causal-signature distance of Stage~D (Section~\ref{sec:moe-drift}).

\section{From Validated Links to a Region-Level Prior}
\label{sec:moe-prior}

The splitter operates on whole source regions, each carrying all six fields at several lags. The validated links of a target are therefore merged into one prior entry per source region. For target $r$, let $\mathcal{E}^{\star}_r$ be its outside links that pass B1 and B2 and are retained by B3. The prior is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_r(r') = \max_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\star}_r:\,\mathrm{src\_region}(e)=r'}\;|\MCI_e|,\qquad r'\ne r,
  \label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
defined for the source regions $r'$ that have at least one validated link. Field and lag detail are kept in the graph file. B4 flags are kept but do not remove regions from the prior. The prior size $|\pi_r|$ is the number of source regions for target $r$; targets with no validated outside link have an empty prior.

\section{The Graph-Guided Mixture-of-Experts Forecaster}
\label{sec:moe-arch}

\subsection{Architecture overview}
The forecasting framework assigns one expert to each target region. Each expert predicts its region's OLR from a small set of source regions chosen by a \emph{splitter}. The splitter plays the role of the gate in a Mixture of Experts~\cite{jacobs1991adaptive,shazeer2017outrageously}: instead of routing an input to an expert, it routes source regions to each target's expert, and it decides which sources are relevant through an invariance criterion. Over time, a region can hold several experts trained in different regimes, and a pool of archived experts can be blended (Chapter~\ref{chap:phase2}). Figure~\ref{fig:moe} shows the architecture and the three points at which the causal graph can enter.

\subsection{Candidate pools}
\label{sec:moe-pools}
Three candidate pools define which source regions the splitter may consider for target $r$: \emph{direct}, the target and its geographically adjacent regions (typically 6--8 regions); \emph{2hop}, adding neighbours of neighbours (about 15--23 regions); and \emph{full}, all 50 regions. The target region is always a candidate, so its own history can be selected, but it is not supplied to the expert unless selected.

\subsection{The invariance-trained splitter}
\label{sec:moe-splitter}
The splitter adapts Discovering Invariant Rationales~\cite{wu2022discovering} from graph classification to regression on a fixed spatial graph. For a target $r$ on day $t$:

\paragraph{Scoring.} The rationale generator computes a score for each candidate edge $r'\to r$ from a 10-day window of the two endpoints' histories,
\begin{equation}
  a_{r'r,t} = \sigma\!\left(\mathrm{MLP}_{\psi}\big[\mathbf{w}^{r'}_t\,\|\,\mathbf{w}^{r}_t\big] + b_{r'r}\right),
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}^{r}_t$ is the flattened window of region $r$'s state over days $t-9,\dots,t$, $\sigma$ is the logistic function, and $b_{r'r}$ is an optional learnable bias (zero unless the initialise mode is used). Using a window rather than a single day lets the generator learn a lagged-association function between the two regions.

\paragraph{Selection.} The top fraction $\rho_{\mathrm{top}}=\min(0.5,\,3/|\mathcal{C}_r|)$ of edges by score forms the causal set $\tilde c_t$, and the remainder forms the non-causal set $\tilde s_t$. Selection is hard, as in the reference implementation~\cite{wu2022discovering}; gradients flow through the scores used as edge weights.

\paragraph{Intervention.} For $M=6$ interventions, the non-causal edges' messages are replaced by those of the same edges on randomly drawn other days $t'_1,\dots,t'_M$, while the causal edges keep day $t$. A shared encoder aggregates the weighted messages and a causal head predicts the target, giving risks $\ell_{t,m}$, $m=1,\dots,M$.

\paragraph{Objective.} The splitter minimises the mean and the variance of the risk across interventions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{split}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\ell_{t,m} \;+\; \lambda(s)\,\var_{m}\big[\ell_{t,m}\big]\;+\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spur}},
  \label{eq:dirloss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda(s)$ is zero for the first 30\% of training steps and then rises linearly to $\lambda_{\max}=1$, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spur}}$ trains a separate spurious head on the non-causal edges with its gradient detached from the generator. The variance term follows the risk-extrapolation principle~\cite{krueger2021out}: if the selected sources truly determine the target, replacing the unselected ones should not change the risk.

\paragraph{Expert.} The expert of region $r$ forms a weighted aggregate of the selected sources' feature vectors (Eq.~\eqref{eq:features}) and maps it to the forecast with a small MLP,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Y}^{r}_{t+L} = \mathrm{MLP}_{\theta_r}\!\Big(\sum_{r'\in\tilde c_t} a_{r'r,t}\,\mathbf{h}^{r'}_t\Big),
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exp}} = \big(\hat{Y}^{r}_{t+L}-Y^r_{t+L}\big)^2 .
  \label{eq:expert}
\end{equation}
The target's own features enter only if the target region is selected. This design makes the forecast depend on the splitter's choice, so that the choice can be evaluated; if the target's own history were always supplied, the expert could ignore the selected sources entirely, because OLR persistence alone is a strong predictor at short leads.

\paragraph{Discrimination.} Whether the splitter actually distinguishes between candidates is measured by the standard deviation $\sigma_a$ of the candidates' mean scores over the test days. A splitter whose scores cluster around one value has $\sigma_a\approx 0$ and is effectively choosing at random. A splitter is called \emph{informative} if $\sigma_a\ge 0.02$, a threshold fixed in the project's earlier experiments, below which the scores are indistinguishable from noise clustered around a single value.

\section{Three Modes of Using the Graph}
\label{sec:moe-modes}

The graph prior $\pi_r$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:prior} can enter the splitter in three ways, each with a different strength of commitment (Figure~\ref{fig:moe}).

\paragraph{(1) Restrict.} The candidate pool is replaced by the prior regions and the target itself:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{restrict}}_r = \{r'\,:\,\pi_r(r')\ \text{defined}\}\cup\{r\}.
  \label{eq:restrict}
\end{equation}
This is a hard constraint. It cannot recover a useful source that the graph missed, but it removes all competition from implausible sources.

\paragraph{(2) Initialise.} The pool is unchanged, and the learnable edge bias of Eq.~\eqref{eq:score} starts at a value proportional to the prior strength:
\begin{equation}
  b^{(0)}_{r'r} = \begin{cases}\beta\,\pi_r(r')/\max_{r''}\pi_r(r'') & r'\in\pi_r,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\beta=2.
  \label{eq:init}
\end{equation}
Prior regions start with higher scores, but training can override the prior.

\paragraph{(3) Regularise.} The pool is unchanged and a penalty discourages scores outside the prior:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{split}} + \gamma\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{off}}_r|}\sum_{r'\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{off}}_r} a_{r'r,t},\qquad
  \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{off}}_r = \{r'\in\mathcal{C}_r: r'\notin\pi_r,\,r'\neq r\},\quad\gamma=1.
  \label{eq:regularise}
\end{equation}
The target region itself is never biased or penalised in any mode.

These three modes span a range from treating the graph as a constraint, through treating it as a starting point, to treating it as a soft preference. They answer different questions: restrict tests whether the graph contains the right sources; initialise tests whether it is a good starting point for learning; regularise tests whether it is a useful inductive bias that data can still override.

\paragraph{A graph as representation, prior, constraint and predictor.} The same object plays four roles that should not be confused. As a scientific \emph{representation}, the graph is a statement about conditional dependence that can be assessed by B1--B4. As a statistical \emph{prior}, it is a belief about which sources are relevant, which data can update. As a computational \emph{constraint}, it reduces the search space of the splitter. Its \emph{predictive utility} is an empirical property of the combination of graph, model and objective. A plausible graph is not automatically a useful prior: an overly restrictive graph removes useful information, a weak graph prior adds noise, and if the splitter's objective rather than its search space is the bottleneck, a random prior of the same size may perform just as well.

\section{Control Priors}
\label{sec:moe-controls}

To attribute any effect to the causal content of the graph, each mode is compared with three controls of matched size. For target $r$ and pool $\mathcal{C}_r$, let $K_r=|\pi_r\cap\mathcal{C}_r|$ be the number of prior regions in the pool.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No prior:} the splitter uses the full pool with no bias or penalty. This measures what the graph adds over the splitter alone.
  \item \textbf{Random prior:} $K_r$ regions drawn uniformly without replacement from $\mathcal{C}_r\setminus\{r\}$, with the PCMCI strengths randomly permuted onto them, using a seed derived from the run seed and the target. This keeps the prior's size and strength distribution and removes its content. If the random prior matches the causal prior, any gain comes from shrinking the pool, not from choosing the right regions.
  \item \textbf{Correlation prior:} the $K_r$ regions with the largest absolute lagged correlation with the target's OLR, computed on training rows only. This tests whether conditioning (causal discovery) adds anything beyond association.
\end{itemize}
All priors have the same size per target, the same pool and the same mode, and all runs use identical data, splits, seeds and training budgets.

\section{Forecast-Time Information Availability}
\label{sec:moe-availability}

A causal link $X^{i}_{t-\tau}\to X^j_t$ found by PCMCI states that the source's value $\tau$ days before the target carries unique information about it. For a direct forecast issued at time $t$ for the target at $t+L$, the latest available source value is at $t$, which is $L$ days before the target. Such a link can therefore be exploited directly only if $\tau\ge L$. If $\tau<L$, the source value that carries the information lies in the future at the issue time; the source region may still be useful if its present state predicts its own future state, but the specific link no longer applies. Formally, the information usable at lead $L$ is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}^{(L)}_j = \{(i,j,\tau)\in\mathcal{E}: \tau\ge L\}.
  \label{eq:availability}
\end{equation}
This condition does not affect the validity of the graph as a description of dependence, but it determines whether the graph is matched to a given forecast lead. A graph whose links are mostly at lags of one or two days is matched to a one-day forecast and not to a seven-day forecast. The remedy is to rebuild the graph with $\taumin=L$, which restricts PCMCI to links usable at the forecast lead.

\section{Temporal Stability of the Causal Structure}
\label{sec:moe-drift}

Stage~D asks whether the causal structure changes over time in a way that tracks known climate regimes. For each target $r$, PCMCI is rerun on sliding windows of $W$ consecutive BSISO seasons ($W=1$ or $3$), with the candidate set restricted to the target's links that passed B1 and B2 plus its own OLR at lags 1--3, so that each window's test is affordable and focused. The window's parent set is the set of significant (source region, field) pairs, $\mathcal{P}_{r,w}$. The causal-signature distance between consecutive windows is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{r,w} = 1 - J\big(\mathcal{P}_{r,w},\mathcal{P}_{r,w-1}\big),
  \label{eq:sigdist}
\end{equation}
and the target-averaged series $\bar\Delta_w=\frac{1}{50}\sum_r\Delta_{r,w}$ is tested for regime shifts with STARS~\cite{rodionov2004sequential}. STARS compares each new value with the mean of the current regime and signals a shift when a regime-shift index, the cumulative normalised deviation from that mean, stays positive over a cut-off length. To relate alarms to climate, a Ni\~{n}o-3.4 proxy is computed from the project's SST field (5\dg S--5\dg N, 170\dg W--120\dg W, season mean, linear trend removed) and each season is labelled El Ni\~{n}o ($>+0.5$ standard deviations), La Ni\~{n}a ($<-0.5$) or neutral. If the causal structure tracks ENSO, $\Delta$ should be larger when the ENSO phase changes between consecutive windows than when it does not, which is tested with a one-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:build-validate} gives the complete build--validate--freeze--use procedure. Every step before line~\ref{line:freeze} uses only the development period.

\caption{Build, validate, freeze and use the causal graph}
\label{alg:build-validate}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require 50- and 100-region node series; season mask; development period $\Ttrain$; test period $\Ttest$
\State Compute BSISO index and season $\Tseason$ (EOF fitted on $\Ttrain$)
\For{each OLR target $j$} \Comment{Stage A}
  \State $\mathcal{E}_j\gets$ PCMCI$(\mathbf{X},\Tseason\cap\Ttrain,j)$ \Comment{Algorithm~\ref{alg:pcmci}}
\EndFor
\State Generate $D$ phase-randomised decoys; run PCMCI on the 550-node pool \Comment{B1}

\State Keep links with $|\MCI|>\kappa_{\mathrm{dec}}$
\For{each link $e$} \Comment{B2}
  \State compute $d$, $s_e$; flag $\tau\ge 25$; mark fail if $s_e>s^{\max}_{v}$
\EndFor
\State Choose $k^\ast$ by $R^2_{\mathrm{val}}$ (fit 1979--2004, score 2005--2012); keep top-$k^\ast$ outside links per target \Comment{B3}
\State Run PCMCI at 100 regions; compute persistence flags and $\Pi_r$ \Comment{B4}
\State Build region prior $\pi_r$ from B1/B2/B3-passing outside links (Eq.~\ref{eq:prior})
\State \textbf{Freeze} graph and prior with all flags and configuration \label{line:freeze}

  \State Train splitter and experts on $\Ttrain$; score on $\Tseason\cap\Ttest$
\EndFor
\State Run sliding-window PCMCI, $\Delta_{r,w}$, STARS and ENSO comparison \Comment{Stage D}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
