\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Weather and climate regimes shift over time. An El Ni\~{n}o year does not behave like a neutral year, and the monsoon's intraseasonal rhythm differs from season to season. A forecasting model trained once on historical data therefore goes stale as conditions change. The usual remedy, retraining the entire model from scratch or on a fixed schedule, is wasteful, because a distribution shift is usually confined to particular regions or relationships rather than the whole system. As forecasting systems grow from regional to global scale, the number of regions that can drift independently grows with them, and so does the cost of full retraining.

A second weakness lies in how forecasting systems choose their inputs. Most select source regions by correlation or by geographic proximity. In the tropical atmosphere, correlations are inflated by autocorrelation and by common drivers such as the large convective envelopes of intraseasonal oscillations~\cite{runge2014quantifying}. A model built on such relationships cannot distinguish "the physics of this region changed" from "a coincidental correlation broke". Causal discovery for time series offers an alternative: it asks whether the past of a source still carries information about a target once the target's other drivers and the source's own memory are accounted for~\cite{runge2018causal,runge2019detecting,runge2019inferring}.

This thesis combines the two ideas. It first discovers and independently validates the causal drivers of each region, and then uses them to guide a Mixture of Experts (MoE) whose experts are adapted only where a measured drift signal says that a region's behaviour has changed.

\section{The Forecasting Setting: BSISO and OLR}
\label{sec:intro-bsiso}

The subseasonal range of two weeks to two months is the least predictable window of weather forecasting and a current research priority~\cite{vitart2017s2s}. In the tropics its main source of skill is organised, propagating convection: the Madden--Julian Oscillation (MJO) in boreal winter~\cite{madden1971detection} and the Boreal Summer Intraseasonal Oscillation (BSISO) between May and October~\cite{wang1997model,lee2013realtime}. The BSISO forms over the equatorial Indian Ocean and moves northward and eastward with a 30--60-day period, driving the active and break spells of the Indian monsoon~\cite{goswami2001intraseasonal}.

The forecast target is outgoing longwave radiation (OLR), the thermal radiation leaving the top of the atmosphere~\cite{liebmann1996description}. Deep convective clouds have cold tops and emit little, so low OLR indicates active convection and high OLR indicates suppressed convection~\cite{arkin1987relationship}. OLR is a convection proxy rather than a rainfall measurement, and it is a demanding target: it has the lowest day-to-day persistence of the six variables used here, so any skill beyond a few days must come from the evolution of the wider system.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a statistically discovered and independently validated causal graph improve how a Mixture-of-Experts forecaster selects its source regions, and can causal-structure drift detection adapt individual regional experts, rather than the whole model, when the climate regime changes?}
\end{quote}

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-objectives}

\paragraph{Phase 1 (this thesis).}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}]
  \item Discover a causal graph, not just a correlation map, across 50 regions and six weather variables, using PCMCI to find which regions' past conditions carry information about another region's future OLR.
  \item Stress-test every discovered link: does it survive decoy variables, is it physically possible at real atmospheric propagation speeds, does it survive trimming to the strongest links, and does it hold when the regions are redrawn at a different resolution?
  \item Feed the validated graph into the forecaster's causal splitter in three ways (narrowing its candidate sources, nudging its initial scores, or discouraging selections outside the graph) and compare it with a splitter without guidance on unseen years.
  \item Build a drift-detection system that watches each region individually and decides whether to retrain that region's expert, spawn a new one for a changed regime, or reactivate an archived expert that has seen the regime before, avoiding full-model retraining.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Phase 2 (extension).}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}, resume]
  \item Design an India-focused hourly forecasting system: a shared graph neural network backbone with region-specific experts conditioned on causally validated drivers, autoregressive forecasts to 72 hours, and station-level validation.
  \item Specify a benchmark against established forecasting models (GraphCast~\cite{lam2023learning} and WeatherNext) and a path to global scale.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:intro-rq}

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.1cm]
  \item[RQ1] Can PCMCI identify meaningful conditional lagged dependencies among six climate variables across 50 regions?
  \item[RQ2] How robust are these dependencies under decoy testing, physical lag checks, top-$k$ selection and a change of spatial resolution?
  \item[RQ3] Does the validated graph improve source selection and forecast skill when used as a splitter prior?
  \item[RQ4] Does a causal-structure drift signal detect regime shifts earlier than an error signal, and does reusing archived experts pay off?
  \item[RQ5] How should these findings shape an India-focused, adaptive forecasting system?
\end{description}

The corresponding testable hypotheses are: (H1) phase-randomised decoys are selected at a rate below 30\% and fewer than 5\% of links violate physical speed limits; (H2) at least half of each target's outside links persist at double resolution for most targets; (H3) the graph prior improves source discrimination and held-out skill; (H4) the structure channel detects known shifts before the error channel; and (H5) recurring regimes allow archived experts to be reactivated rather than retrained from scratch.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

Phase~1 forecasts regional-mean OLR anomalies directly at 7-day (primary) and 1-day leads on a 2.5\dg\ tropical grid (30\dg S--30\dg N, all longitudes) whose BSISO-active core is the Indo-Pacific. Graph discovery uses linear, lagged dependence; contemporaneous links are excluded. All links are statistical statements about conditional dependence between region means, not proofs of physical mechanism. Phase~2 is specified as a design; global forecasting is future work.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contrib}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{build--validate--freeze--use} protocol in which the causal graph is built and validated on 1979--2012 only and frozen before any test year is used.
  \item A \textbf{vectorised PCMCI} that matches the Tigramite reference and processes 18{,}000 candidates per target for 50 targets in about 23 minutes.
  \item A \textbf{validated causal graph} for BSISO-season OLR: 536 links, 0 of 250 decoys selected, 3 links physically implausible, and 62\% mean persistence at double resolution.
  \item A \textbf{graph-guided splitter} whose prior makes full-pool source scores informative (0 $\to$ 24 regions at 7 days, 0 $\to$ 36 at 1 day) and improves 1-day skill in 32 of 50 regions.
  \item A \textbf{dual-channel drift detector and expert lifecycle}: the causal-structure channel detects known shifts several times sooner than the error channel at two of three regions, and 104 of 171 regime changes reuse an archived expert.
  \item A \textbf{Phase~2 design} that transfers these components to India-focused hourly forecasting.
\end{enumerate}
